## Draft prose

Before evaluating whether transfer learning improves commodity forecasting in data-sparse markets, it is necessary to establish what classical, single-market methods already achieve when data is *not* scarce. This benchmark matters twofold: classical models supply the target-only baselines against which the proposed framework is compared (Section 4.5), and their known limitations — even under the most favourable data conditions of deep, liquid, developed-market exchanges — motivate the case for knowledge transfer in the first place.

The dominant empirical tradition regresses commodity returns on macroeconomic and financial predictors and evaluates the resulting forecasts out of sample against a no-predictability benchmark. Gargano and Timmermann (2014), using Reuters/Jefferies-CRB commodity indexes over 1947–2010, find that predictability from variables such as industrial production growth, commodity-currency exchange rates, and global economic activity indices is present but highly heterogeneous: it is strongest for industrial, metals, and textile sub-indices and weakest for food, livestock, and fats/oils; it is concentrated at the quarterly horizon; and it strengthens markedly during recessions. Multivariate shrinkage methods (ridge regression, complete subset regression) improve on univariate forecasts by 5–14% in mean squared error for the commodities where predictability exists, but deliver essentially no improvement elsewhere. Guidolin and Pedio (2021) extend this line by asking whether commodity-*specific* factors (basis, hedging pressure, momentum) add value beyond macro factors alone. Statistically, the answer is largely no — an AR(1) benchmark beats stepwise macro-factor models on out-of-sample error for 12 of 15 commodities — yet economically, adding commodity-specific factors to a stepwise-selected portfolio strategy roughly doubles the annualised Sharpe ratio relative to the macro-only strategy (0.65 versus 0.36), with the gap widening sharply in high-volatility regimes. Guidolin and Ionta (2026) report a structurally similar pattern for climate predictors: none of their stepwise or regime-switching specifications beat an AR(1) benchmark on statistical loss, yet a climate-augmented hidden-Markov model produces the largest gains in certainty-equivalent portfolio return across all levels of risk aversion — though the associated confidence intervals are wide enough to often include zero.

This recurring divergence between statistical accuracy and economic value is arguably the single most important methodological lesson this cluster of literature offers the thesis: a model that fails to beat a naive benchmark on RMSE can still be economically informative, and the reverse can also hold. It directly anticipates the dual evaluation strategy already specified in Sections 4.6 and 5.6 (statistical metrics alongside a trading-strategy simulation), and cautions against treating a single loss function as sufficient evidence that transfer learning has, or has not, added value.

A second strand models volatility rather than returns. Nguyen and Walther (2018) decompose commodity futures volatility into short- and long-run components using GARCH-MIDAS, linking the long-run component to macro-financial drivers (industrial production, consumer sentiment, economic policy uncertainty) across a 1996–2015 sample of WTI, Brent, gold, silver, and platinum. Two findings are relevant beyond volatility modelling narrowly construed: first, no single specification dominates across commodities or forecast horizons, echoing the same model-instability theme found in the return-forecasting literature; second, the identified drivers vary systematically by commodity type — oil responds to demand shocks within one quarter, while metals respond only after four to seven quarters — indicating that even within a single, data-rich developed market, "commodities" are not a homogeneous forecasting problem. This heterogeneity is worth flagging early, since it foreshadows a design question the thesis must confront in Chapter 3: whether a single source model can meaningfully serve multiple commodity classes, or whether commodity-specific source models are required.

A third, older tradition evaluates purely technical or trend-following signals rather than fundamentals-based predictors. Auer (2021), examining fifty years of S&P GSCI futures (1970–2019), finds that cross-sectional momentum retains statistically significant predictive power with no systematic decline over time — in contrast to the well-documented decay of similar signals in equity markets — while time-series momentum performs close to chance for most commodities. This result is a useful, if narrow, counterpoint: it shows that at least one class of simple, low-data-requirement forecasting rule remains viable in commodity markets over long horizons, which is relevant when considering what a data-scarce target market could realistically implement without any transfer at all.

Sun et al. (2023) provide a broader review of classical (regression, grey-system, ARIMA/GARCH-family) and hybrid methods specifically for agricultural commodity prices, drawing overwhelmingly on Chinese-market applications. Their one point of direct relevance to the data-scarcity theme of this thesis is the observation that grey models (e.g., GM(1,1)) are specifically suited to "forecasting problems where data are scarce or incomplete" — the closest any paper in the classical-forecasting literature comes to addressing data scarcity on its own terms, albeit as a generic small-sample technique rather than a developing-economy-specific one.

Taken together, this literature establishes three points the thesis can build on directly. First, classical single-market models — even with several decades of clean, developed-market data — produce forecasts that are horizon-dependent, commodity-specific, and frequently indistinguishable from a random-walk or AR(1) benchmark on statistical grounds, while still generating economically meaningful gains in some settings. This sets a realistic, not inflated, bar for what the proposed transfer-learning framework needs to beat. Second, no classical study in this cluster pools information *across markets*: each is estimated on a single country's (almost always the United States') exchange data, using that market's own long history. The entire tradition is, by construction, silent on what happens when that history is short — which is precisely the condition the target economies in this thesis face. Third, the statistical-versus-economic-value divergence documented repeatedly here is not a peripheral detail but a design constraint: any claim in Chapter 5 that transfer learning "improves forecasting" must specify on which loss function, echoing the caution these papers apply to themselves.

## Flags — evidence not yet in the library

The thesis structure commits Section 2.1 to two additional strands that the current corpus does not support, and I have not fabricated citations for either:

1. **Structural supply-demand / fundamentals models.** No paper in the library estimates a structural commodity-market model (storage/convenience-yield models, supply-response models, or similar). This is a standard classical-forecasting strand and its absence is conspicuous — worth sourcing before this section is finalized (e.g., work in the tradition of Deaton and Laroque on storage models, or Pindyck on the economics of commodity price cycles, though these need to be independently verified and added to the project library rather than cited from memory).
2. **Boom-bust cycles and non-stationarity.** Related to the above — no paper here directly addresses commodity price non-stationarity, unit roots, or long-run boom-bust dynamics as a forecasting challenge. Byrne et al. (2020) and Alquist et al. (2020), reviewed for Section 2.4, touch structural breaks and comovement but were written for a different purpose (comovement decomposition) and would need reframing, not just relocation, if used here.

Until these are sourced, the section as drafted covers the *econometric-predictor* and *technical-signal* strands of classical forecasting solidly, but is missing the *structural-model* strand the outline calls for. I'd suggest either sourcing 1–2 papers on this before finalizing, or narrowing the section's stated scope to match what is actually covered (a decision worth flagging to your advisor either way, since it changes what the section promises to deliver).
